D4 opens the model up. If it keeps a picture of the board inside, a simple classifier should be able to read the board from its activations, and editing that picture should change its moves. Two checks keep the reading honest: a control task, and the causal edit.

13.1The idea, and the number it was checked against

The model only ever reads move tokens. After every move, D4 records the vector after each layer (Lesson 9) and trains a probe, a linear classifier, to read from that vector, for each of the 64 squares, whether it is mine, theirs or empty, relative to the player who just moved. If the probe reads the board well, the board is encoded in the model. This is the tradition of Li et al. (2023) and Nanda et al. (2023), who showed Othello-GPT stores the board, and stores it as mine/theirs.

Before building on it, the team reproduced a published number with its own code (the reproduction gate, reproduce_nanda_probe.py): linear probes on the synthetic Othello-GPT, trained on 100,000 games.

The key finding reproduces: a linear probe reads the board almost perfectly as mine/theirs and only about 75% as black/white. The one miss, black/white at layer 0, is flat at about 76% across all layers in our run, which points to a difference in what “layer 0” means; it does not affect D4, which uses mine/theirs.

13.2Data, activations and the probe

  • Three sets of games, never mixed.From 11,000 validation games of the model’s own distribution, the first 10,000 fit the probes and the last 1,000 choose the layer. 1,000 test games give the score, so the test games never influence any choice.
  • Every layer, every position.The vector after each block (transformer, Mamba) or each LSTM layer, at every real position of every game.
  • Standardized first.Each activation feature is shifted and scaled by its mean and spread over the fitting games. LSTM hidden states lie between −1 and 1 while residual streams are much larger, so without this one learning rate would be unfair to some architectures. Checked on a model whose layer holds the true board: the probe reads it at 0.9999.
  • One linear map per layer.From the width of the layer to 64 squares × 3 classes, trained with cross-entropy and AdamW (learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 0.01) for 2 epochs, one step per 1,024 positions: about 1,100 steps.
  • 219–220Activations for 256 games at a time, computed under no_grad: the model is only read, never trained.
  • 221–223The real labels from mine_theirs, the control labels (section 13.3), and the standardized vectors, all at the real positions only.
  • 224–237Shuffle the positions and take steps of 1,024. Each step trains, for every layer, the real probe on the real labels and the control probe on the control labels, from the same vectors. The MLP probes (section 13.6) have their own optimizer, so adding them left the linear probes, and the score, exactly as before.

13.3The control task: how much can a probe score by memorizing?

A probe with enough capacity can learn things the model does not represent in any useful way, simply by memorizing. Hewitt and Liang (2019) proposed a control: give the probe labels that have no link to the board, and see how well it does anyway. The real probe’s accuracy minus the control probe’s accuracy is the selectivity, the part of the reading that memorizing cannot explain.

Real labels · from the board

Two games both have E3 played as move 4. Their boards differ, so the real labels differ: the probe must read the board out of the vector to get them right.

Control labels · from the type only

A position’s type is (square just played, position in the game): 60 × 59 = 3,540 types. Each type gets one fixed random label per square, drawn from that square’s real mix of classes. Both games above get the same control labels, so a control probe can only score by recognizing types.

  • 126–129The type of every position: token of the square just played × 59 + position.
  • 132–135How often each square is empty, mine or theirs in the fitting games.
  • 138–144One uniform random number per type and square, turned into a class by that square’s cumulative frequencies, with seed 0: the control labels match the real class mix, but carry no information about any particular board.

13.4Choosing the layer, and the score

  • 537–541Class frequencies and control labels from the fitting games, the standardization statistics, then all probes.
  • 543–547Accuracy of every probe on the layer-choice games and on the test games, and the selectivity of every layer on both.
  • 548The best layer is the one with the highest selectivity on the layer-choice games, never on test.
  • 549The occupancy-only baseline: the accuracy of a rule that knows only which squares are occupied (empty squares called empty, occupied ones given their most common class). The move list alone gives this much away.

The score is the best layer’s selectivity on the test games. Every other number goes into details, per layer: probe accuracy, control accuracy, selectivity, the gain over layer 0, accuracy on occupied squares only (whose disc it is, which the moves do not give away), and each layer’s gain over the occupancy baseline.

13.6The causal check: does the model use what the probe reads?

A board that can be read from the activations is not necessarily a board the model uses. The probe might pick up a by-product. Li et al. (2023) settled this for Othello-GPT by editing its internal board and watching its moves follow. D4 does the same on every model, against a control edit of the same size.

  • 350–353Work in the standardized space, with a step length of 0.05 × √width, so a step means the same for every architecture.
  • 356–361Stop moving a case as soon as the probe reads the whole edited board and gives the flipped square’s new class at least 0.99. Edits stay as small as that allows.
  • 362–367Otherwise take one step down the gradient of the probe’s loss for the edited board, normalized to unit length, and only for the cases not yet done.
Anchor (test)Edited layersCausal score [95% CI]Probe edit · random · noneLabel
Othello-GPT synthetic4 to 70.693 [0.665, 0.719]0.872 · 0.179 · 0.198decodable and causal
Othello-GPT championship3 to 70.151 [0.122, 0.180]0.544 · 0.393 · 0.387decodable but not causal

The synthetic Othello-GPT is the positive control, because the published work already showed its board is causal: editing it moves boundary accuracy from 0.18 to 0.87. The championship model’s board is readable (selectivity 0.27) but editing it barely beats a random push, so it is labelled decodable but not causal. H3 reports such models separately and tests whether the label predicts fragility beyond the selectivity score.

Why edit from the middle layer onward? The setting was chosen on the anchors only, as the one with the largest causal score on the positive control. Editing only the best layer (6 of 8) comes too late: later layers still attend to earlier positions that hold the unedited board, and the synthetic model builds its board around layer 4. Editing from layer 0 adds noise where no board is held yet. The 0.20 pass mark was written down before that comparison was run and not changed after it.
