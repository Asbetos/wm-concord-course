2.1The board and the opening

Othello is played by two players, Black and White, on an 8 by 8 board. All the discs are the same: black on one side, white on the other. Columns are lettered A to H and rows numbered 1 to 8, so every square has a name such as D3. In this repo row 1 is at the top, so A1 is the top-left corner (square 0 in the code) and H8 the bottom-right (square 63).

Every game starts from the same four discs in the centre: White on D4 and E5, Black on E4 and D5. Black moves first.

2.2A move: trap and flip

A move puts one disc, your colour up, on an empty square. It is legal only if it traps at least one opponent disc: in a straight line from the new disc (across, up or down, or diagonal) there must be one or more opponent discs and then one of your own. Every trapped disc flips to your colour. If the new disc traps lines in several directions, all of them flip.

2.3Passing and the end of the game

If the player to move has no legal move, they pass and the other player moves again. The game ends when neither player can move, almost always because all 64 squares are full after 60 moves. The player with more discs wins.

In the project’s test data, 98.8% of the random (synthetic) games and 96% of the championship games run the full 60 moves. Passes are not rare: about a third of the random games and 58% of the championship games contain at least one, and in random games the pass often comes just before the final move. A position offers about 8.5 legal moves on average.

Why passes matter to the code

The game files store only the squares played, never the passes. So when one player moves twice in a row, the code has to work out that the other player passed. Lesson 3 shows how replay() does it.

2.4Try it

The dots always show the legal moves for whoever is to move. Point at a dot to see which discs it would flip, then click to play it. Random move picks uniformly among the legal moves, which is exactly how the synthetic training games were made.

2.5Why Othello suits this project

  • The truth is exact.The rules are fixed and nothing is random, so a move list determines the board completely. That gives every test its answer key.
  • The board is hidden inside the move list.Which squares are occupied is easy: discs are never removed, so a square stays occupied once it has been played. Whose disc sits there is hard, because it depends on every flip since. This is why the project’s tests ask about ownership, and why D4 reports an occupancy-only baseline.
  • Mine and theirs.The code describes each board two ways: black / white, and mine / theirs relative to the player who just moved. Nanda et al. (2023) found Othello-GPT stores the board the second way: a linear probe reads mine / theirs at about 99%, but black / white at only about 75%. The team reproduced this before building on it (Lesson 13).
  • Strategy doesn’t matter, but it shapes the data.The models only learn which moves are legal, not how to win. Championship players do try to win, so their games look different from random ones, and that difference is one of the shifts the fragility suite tests (Lessons 4 and 14).
